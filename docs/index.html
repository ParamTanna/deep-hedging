Can a neural network learn when not to trade?

Param Tanna (24110236) and Pranav Magarde (24110269), ES 335 Machine Learning, IIT Gandhinagar.
Code, notebook and every result file: github.com/ParamTanna/deep-hedging

We sell a 30-day option on a stock index and hedge it once a day, paying a fee on every trade. Black-Scholes says how much to hold only if trading is free, so we train networks on simulated markets to minimise the worst 5% of outcomes directly. In simulation they beat every classic hedging rule at every fee level. On real NIFTY 50 and S&P 500 options sold from 2019 on, networks trained on our calibrated simulator cut the tail loss of delta hedging by to ; their lead over the best tuned rule is smaller and only partly significant. And what they learned can be read off: the plain network hedges like delta at a higher volatility, and the band network's no-trade width grows as cost, faster than the textbook cube root, which turns out to be right for daily trading.

Hedge one option yourself

You have just sold the call. Each day, look at the index and choose how much of it to hold until tomorrow, between 0 and 1 units. Every change costs 10 bps of what you trade. After 30 days the option settles and your result is set next to Black-Scholes delta, the Whalley-Wilmott band and our trained network on the same path. Enter advances a day.

1Problem and research question

A desk sells one call option on an index. The buyer pays a premium today; in 30 trading days the desk owes \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\), where \(S_T\) is the index at expiry and \(K\) the strike, and that bill has no ceiling. Left unhedged, the average of the worst 5% of outcomes on our simulated test set is a loss of per 100 of notional, about times the premium collected.

The textbook fix is delta hedging: hold \(\Delta = \partial C/\partial S\) units of the index so that small moves cancel. With continuous, free trading this removes the risk entirely. Real desks trade at discrete times, so risk leaks between trades, and every trade pays a fee. Trading more often shrinks the first problem and inflates the second, and once fees exist there is no formula for the best strategy.

Research question. For a short 30-day at-the-money call on an equity index, rebalanced daily under a proportional fee, which hedging policy has the best tail outcome, measured by CVaR at 95% of final profit and loss after all fees? And does a neural policy trained only on that objective learn the no-trade behaviour that cost-aware rules build in by hand?

Where this sits in the ML loop. Usually a model outputs a score and a threshold turns it into an action. Here the output is the action and the loss is the P&L itself, so we train on exactly what we care about. The price is a market simulator, which is a model of the data, not the data.
  • Unit of analysis: one episode, an option sold on one day and hedged to expiry. Inside it the policy makes 30 decisions, one per trading day.
  • Target: none in the supervised sense, because nobody knows the right hedge under costs. A policy is judged by the distribution of its final P&L over many episodes.
  • Known at each decision: prices up to today, the current holding, time left, the volatility quoted at the sale, and recent realised volatility. Never a future price; a test changes future prices and checks that no earlier decision moves.

The claim is narrow on purpose: one contract, proportional fees, zero interest and dividends, a volatility quote estimated from past returns because we have no option market data, and about seven years of real test history. Section 8 spells out what that rules out.

2Data source and description

Daily history of the NIFTY 50 (^NSEI) and the S&P 500 (^GSPC) from Yahoo Finance's public chart endpoint, fetched by src/data.py with no key or login. One row is one trading day; we use only the date and the adjusted close.

Five things the quality checks found
  • NIFTY starts in September 2007 on Yahoo, not 2000 as our proposal assumed, which shortens the Indian training era to about eleven years.
  • Missing NIFTY rows are exchange holidays that Yahoo emits as empty rows, one of them 27 November 2008 during the Mumbai attacks. A price that never existed cannot be imputed, so these rows are dropped.
  • The longest gaps are real closures. The S&P gap ending 17 September 2001 is the closure after 9/11; the return across it is one genuine four-day move and stays in.
  • Adjusted close equals close because both series are price indices, so dividends are not in the data. That matches our zero-dividend model and is a limitation.
  • NIFTY volume is mostly zero early on, which is Yahoo not reporting it. We never use volume.

Two statistics decide almost everything downstream, and both reject the simplest model of prices, geometric Brownian motion (GBM), which treats daily returns as independent normal draws.

Fat tails. Excess kurtosis is 0 for a normal distribution; before 2019 it is for NIFTY and for the S&P. Volatility clusters. The Ljung-Box statistic on absolute returns over 20 lags is for NIFTY and for the S&P, against a 5% critical value of 31.4. On raw returns it is only for NIFTY, which does not matter here because we never forecast direction. Calm days follow calm days and violent days follow violent ones, and for a hedger that is the fact that matters most.

3Preprocessing and statistical reasoning

  1. Clean. Sort, drop duplicates and missing or non-positive closes.
  2. Log returns \(r_t = \log(P_t/P_{t-1})\) of the adjusted close. They add over time and do not depend on the price level, so a 24,000-point index and a 6,000-point index can share one model.
  3. Split by time at 1 January 2019, before anything is fitted. Everything estimated from data uses returns up to 2018; every real option sold from 2019 on is test data. A random split would let the simulator learn from days lying between test days.
  4. One episode protocol for every source. 60 trading days of history, then 30 of hedging. At the sale the desk quotes the volatility of the 60-day history, prices the option with Black-Scholes at that volatility and starts hedging. Real, GBM and Heston windows all run through this same code.
  5. Rescale each path to 100 at the sale, strike 100, so P&L is per 100 of notional and comparable across indices.
Where leakage could hide. Network inputs are scaled by each episode's own quoted volatility, computed from its own past. No test-period statistic enters any scaling. A January 2019 option uses late-2018 history, which a desk would know.

Why we need a simulator

Training needs hundreds of thousands of episodes; the longer series holds only about 220 non-overlapping 30-day windows. So policies train on simulated paths and are tested on real history the simulator never saw. In the GBM world each path's volatility is drawn from the trailing 60-day volatilities seen before 2019 (5% to 74%, 2008 included), and the desk is told the true volatility, so delta is the known right answer at zero cost. In the Heston world volatility is random and mean-reverting, \(dv = \kappa(\theta - v)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v}\,dW_2\), correlated with the price by \(\rho\), and the desk must quote it from history, as on real data.

Four objects we keep apart: the market, our sample of it, the simulator fitted to the sample, and the policy trained on the simulator. Mistaking the third for the first is the main risk, so every policy is tested on real history and the sim-to-real gap is reported as a result.

The simulator we threw away

Our first Heston fit matched the daily moments everyone checks first and looked fine. It was wrong where it matters. Over one day, a delta-hedged short option makes roughly

$$\text{P\&L}_t \;\approx\; \tfrac12\,\Gamma_t S_t^2\left(\sigma_{\text{quoted}}^2\,\Delta t \;-\; r_t^2\right),$$

so losses are driven by how far realised volatility strays from the quote over the option's life. Matching NIFTY's daily kurtosis pushed vol-of-vol \(\xi\) to about 2, and volatility then swung far too much over a month: the 95th-percentile surprise was times the quote against in real data, and plain delta hedging had a tail loss of against a real .

So we refitted to the statistic that matters: the distribution of \(\log(\sigma_{\text{realised}}/\sigma_{\text{quoted}})\) over 30-day windows and its correlation with the window's return. Vol-of-vol fell to for NIFTY and for the S&P, both fits satisfy the Feller condition, and on something never fitted, the tail loss of delta hedging, the new simulator gives against a real for NIFTY and against for the S&P.

Parameter table and fitting details

The comparison above is still on the calibration period, so we treat it as a sanity check rather than evidence; the evidence is the 2019 test. For the S&P, several optimiser starts reached nearly the same objective with different \((\kappa, \xi)\) pairs: the two are only identified jointly, along a ridge. For NIFTY the fit converged to the same point from every start.

4Baselines and methods

Three facts from first principles shape everything here. Delta hedging leaves an error that shrinks like \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) as you rebalance more often. Fees grow like \(1/\sqrt{\Delta t}\), so hedging continuously is infinitely expensive. And it pays to trade when the hedge has drifted rather than on a clock: with continuous trading the best tolerated drift grows like \(\text{cost}^{1/3}\), the Whalley-Wilmott band.

The derivations, in four short steps

1. What delta cancels. Over one step \(dC = \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \Theta\,dt\). Holding \(\Delta\) units removes the linear term, and Black-Scholes sets \(\Theta = -\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2 S^2\), which leaves the expression in section 3. Per step the variance of \(r_t^2 - \sigma^2\Delta t\) is \(2\sigma^4\Delta t^2\) for normal returns; over \(T/\Delta t\) steps the error's standard deviation scales like \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). Our engine reproduces this: from 30 to 120 rebalances the tail goes from \(-1.006\) to \(-0.506\), a ratio of 1.99 against 2.

2. Why continuous hedging is infinitely expensive. Each step delta moves by about \(\Gamma\,dS\), so a trade is of order \(\Gamma S\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\) and costs \(k\) times its value. Over \(T/\Delta t\) steps the bill grows like \(k\,\Gamma S^2\sigma\,T/\sqrt{\Delta t}\).

3. Trade when it matters. Let the holding drift up to \(H\) from delta. The risk carried costs about \(\gamma\sigma^2S^2H^2\); the holding hits the edge at a rate proportional to \((\Gamma\sigma S)^2/H\), paying \(kS\) per unit traded. Minimising the sum,

$$\frac{d}{dH}\Big[\gamma\sigma^2S^2H^2 + \frac{kS\,\Gamma^2\sigma^2S^2}{H}\Big] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; H \propto \Big(\frac{k\,S\,\Gamma^2}{\gamma}\Big)^{1/3}.$$

The band is widest where gamma is large, near the strike and close to expiry. This assumes the holding drifts smoothly into the edge, which needs continuous trading. With one decision a day, delta can jump past a narrow band in a single step; then the choice is made step by step (keep a gap \(x\) at a risk of order \(x^2\) for a day, or pay \(k|x|\) to close it), which puts the threshold at a multiple of \(k\) itself. Section 6 measures which regime we are in.

4. Why learn anything. Whalley-Wilmott is an asymptotic answer for small costs, exponential utility, continuous monitoring and known volatility. Ours is CVaR of a 30-step daily hedge with a guessed volatility. Nobody has a formula for that, so we optimise it directly.

Learning a policy without labels

A network maps the state on a rebalance day to a holding. We simulate a batch of 8,192 episodes, let the network make all 30 decisions, and compute the final P&L with the same accounting engine that scores the baselines. Every step of that calculation is differentiable, so the gradient of the risk of the final P&L flows back through all 30 decisions (Buehler et al., 2019). The objective is CVaR at 95% in the Rockafellar-Uryasev form,

$$\operatorname{CVaR}_{0.95}(L) = \min_{w}\Big\{\,w + \frac{1}{0.05}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(L - w)^+\big]\Big\}, \qquad L = -\text{P\&L},$$

with \(w\) set on every batch to that batch's 95% quantile, its exact minimiser, so each step optimises exactly the batch CVaR. We chose CVaR over variance because variance punishes a surprise profit as hard as a surprise loss, and a hedger does not. Each network trains for 1,500 steps of Adam with cosine decay; the checkpoint with the best validation CVaR is kept.

Why not reinforcement learning? Same goal, different gradient. RL estimates gradients from rewards it cannot differentiate; our simulator is differentiable, so we get the exact gradient through every decision.

Three networks, each for a reason

  • MLP (3 layers of 64). It sees the current holding and nothing about bands, so any no-trade behaviour it shows, it found itself. This model answers the research question.
  • No-trade band network (Imaki et al., 2021). It outputs a band from the state and the hedge is the current holding clamped into it. The band shape is built in; only its width is learned.
  • GRU. Under stochastic volatility the variance cannot be observed, so the GRU reads the return history and can carry its own estimate of the regime. Used only where volatility moves.
Inputs, and why the network is small

Inputs are written in the units the problem is invariant to: moneyness in remaining standard deviations \(\log(S/K)/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})\), the square root of the fraction of life left, total quoted volatility \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), and the current holding. Heston-trained models also see how far recent realised volatility has moved from the quote. Holdings stay in \([0,1]\), the range of delta itself.

The policy maps four or five numbers to one, and the function is smooth. Overfitting is not the worry, since every step sees fresh simulated paths; the question is only whether more capacity helps, and an ablation over widths 8 to 128 answers it (section 6).

5Evaluation strategy

Everything below was fixed in code before any test number existed, including the cost levels, which are part of the problem rather than a knob to turn afterwards.

  • Training: fresh simulated episodes at every step.
  • Validation: 20,000 fixed simulated episodes (seed 1001). It picks every checkpoint, the band width and the Whalley-Wilmott risk aversion.
  • Simulated test: 100,000 fixed episodes (seed 2002), scored once.
  • Real test: every option sold from 1 January 2019, on NIFTY and on the S&P, scored once. Real windows before 2019 are scored too, but only as a diagnostic, since the simulator was fitted on them.

Fair comparisons. Every strategy is scored by one accounting engine on the same paths with the same cost and premium, and rules with a parameter are tuned on the same validation set the competing network was selected on. Metric. CVaR at 95% of final P&L after all fees, per 100 of notional; fees are inside it, so trading constantly cannot look good. We also report the mean, standard deviation, fees and number of trades. Uncertainty. Paired bootstrap intervals, so a difference reflects the strategies and not which paths were drawn; on real data neighbouring windows share 29 of 30 days, so we resample blocks of 30.

The ten controls every result had to pass first

src/controls.py checks that delta and gamma agree with finite differences to \(10^{-8}\); that at zero cost the tail shrinks with rebalancing and follows the \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) law; that delta hedging's mean P&L is statistically zero; that a zero-width band is delta and a very wide band never trades; that Leland and Whalley-Wilmott reduce to delta at zero cost; and that changing future prices never moves a past decision, for every rule and every network. The strongest control is for the network itself: trained at zero cost, it should land close to delta.

What the MLP learned: delta at a higher volatility

The MLP is never told about bands. In the explorer below its resting holding (dashed red) sits on a flatter curve than delta, the shape delta takes when volatility is higher. Fitted to one effective volatility, it hedges as if volatility were times the truth at zero cost, rising to times at 50 bps.

The zero-cost part surprised us: the network stays close to delta there (correlation ) yet beats its tail by . Hedging at a higher volatility costs a little on every path and pays back on paths that stay near the strike, exactly where daily rebalancing leaks most. With costs, the inflation grows linearly in the fee, which is Leland's direction. A one-line rule built from this reading, Leland's delta at times the volatility, recovers of the MLP's advantage at 10 bps. The rest is inertia: the MLP leaves part of the gap to its target open, more as cost rises.

The equation behind this, and how close it is to Leland

Hedge at \(\hat\sigma > \sigma\) after selling at \(\sigma\), and the final P&L is the premium gap \(V(\sigma) - V(\hat\sigma)\), a fixed cost, plus \(\tfrac12(\hat\sigma^2-\sigma^2)\int S^2\,\Gamma_{\hat\sigma}\,dt\), which is large on paths that spend their life near the strike, plus the daily-hedging error. The mean is unchanged; the tail shrinks. On the validation set, delta at \(\sigma \times c\) has its best tail near \(c \approx 1.1\) to \(1.15\), right where the network sits, and recovers of the network's zero-cost gain.

The cost part grows in proportion to the fee at every volatility we checked, like Leland's, but hardly depends on the level of volatility, where Leland's falls like \(1/\sigma\). So the network found Leland's direction, not Leland's formula. The rule above recovers of the advantage at 50 bps, where inertia matters more. The scaling factor was read off the network's holdings, never tuned on P&L, and all of this uses the validation set only.

The band network, and the cube-root law

The band network's learned half-width at the money grows from at 5 bps to at 50 bps, a power law with exponent (standard error ), not the cube root of section 4. We suspected the network first, so we refitted the two rules whose widths were tuned separately at each cost, with no network involved: for the constant band and for Whalley-Wilmott. The reason is the one flagged in section 4: at the money, delta moves about in one day, more than the whole band, so the band is crossed by jumps and the trade-off is made step by step. The measured exponents sit between the continuous-time 1/3 and the one-step 1. The network found the law of the problem it was actually given.

The one day the simulator never showed the networks

On NIFTY's calibration era the Heston-trained networks trail delta, and of their worst windows were sold in 2008 or 2009 ( for delta). The very worst contain 18 May 2009, the first trading day after the general election result, when NIFTY rose in one session, the largest move in our data. The day before, the network held of the index where delta held : the flatter hedge that helps everywhere else left it short when an in-the-money option jumped, at a cost of about per 100 of notional. Heston prices move continuously and practically never jump like that, so the networks never learned to fear it. Jumps are the first thing we would add to the simulator.

Four ways this result could mislead

Aggregation. An average can hide where a gain comes from, so we split by regime and read the worst episodes one by one; the gain is concentrated in volatile months, and in calm ones the MLP is worse than delta. The metric. A gain bought by shifting the mean would show up in the mean, fees and spread we report next to the tail, and at zero cost every mean is zero. Training on a quadratic loss gives a clearly worse tail, so the objective is doing the work. Shortcuts. Decisions use prices up to that day only, which a test checks by changing future prices; the split is by time and the test sets were scored once. Capacity and luck. Seeds move the result by at most , intervals are paired and block-resampled on real data, and where an interval includes zero we say so.

8Limitations

No option market data. We quote volatility from the last 60 days of returns, while a real desk sells at implied volatility, usually higher. That shifts every strategy's P&L alike, so our claims are about controlling the tail of a hedged position, not about whether selling options pays.

No jumps in the simulator. Heston moves continuously, and 18 May 2009 shows what that costs. Real volatility clustering also decays more slowly than a one-factor model allows.

One contract, one instrument, one kind of cost. An at-the-money 30-day call, rebalanced daily, hedged with the index alone, with a proportional fee: no bid-ask dynamics, no market impact, no second option to hedge gamma with.

Short real history. Seven years of overlapping monthly windows are few independent observations, and March 2020 dominates the real tail. Most real-data comparisons against the best rule are not statistically decisive.

A fixed training recipe. Learning rate, schedule and the 1,500-step budget were set once. The seed and width ablations suggest the results are not fragile to them, but we did not test every choice.

9Conclusion

Learning a hedge on the tail objective beats the rules where it was trained. In simulation, at every cost from 5 to 50 bps, both networks beat the best rule with intervals that exclude zero, and the lead grows with cost.

What the networks learned can be read and checked. The MLP hedges like delta at a higher volatility, a margin that protects the tail even at zero cost plus a cost adjustment in Leland's direction. The band network learned a band that widens faster than the textbook cube root, as theory predicts for once-a-day trading, and the tuned rules agree.

On real markets the simulator decides the outcome. Networks trained on our calibrated Heston simulator cut the tail loss of delta hedging by to on options sold from 2019 on and lead the best rule by (S&P) and (NIFTY), a lead that is statistically clear for one network per index. Trained on plain GBM, the same networks transfer much less well. Their clearest failure, a single jump in 2009, names the next steps: jumps in the simulator, then implied volatility data, then an option as a second hedging instrument.
